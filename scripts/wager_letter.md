# The trickle-down wager comes due

On {frozen_on}, a page in this repo made a falsifiable claim and picked a date.
The date is today.

**The claim.** A lineup-bottom model — the cheapest model its vendor ships —
would match {baseline}, the frontier as of June 2026, on one of two yardsticks:
METR's 50% time horizon, or the Artificial Analysis intelligence index at no
more than a tenth of {baseline}'s price.

**The two dates.** {method_b_date} from the price-decline method,
{method_a_date} from the historical-lag method. The earlier of the two is today.
Run at the slowest trend Epoch AI has fitted, the price-decline method gave
{slow_date} instead.

Two illustrative scenarios, not a forecast. They landed close together because
capability diffusion and price decline are two views of the same process — that
was never two independent estimates agreeing, and neither date was good to
better than a season. The historical-lag method rested on {n_pairs} pairs
produced by only {n_matchers} catch-up releases.

**Where it stood when the wager was frozen.** {baseline} scored
{baseline_aa} on the Artificial Analysis intelligence index. The best any
bottom-tier model managed was {closest} at {closest_aa} — {gap} points short.
METR had measured exactly zero bottom-tier models, which is why the wager
resolves on the index rather than on time horizon.

**Settle it.** The terms are in `wager.json`; nothing about them is negotiable
now, which was the point of writing them down first. Note that the index arm is
a joint event — the index AND a tenth of the price, on the same model, at the
same time. Matching the index alone does not settle it.

Two ways this ends badly, both worth checking for before declaring a winner.
The wager could resolve YES on a technicality — an index version change that
rescored everything, or a vendor relabelling a mid-tier model as its lineup
bottom. Or it could resolve NO for the wrong reason: the trend held perfectly
well and the bottom tier simply stopped being called the bottom tier.
